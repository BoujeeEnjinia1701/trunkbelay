# TrunkBelay STEM check

Verdict: the working principle of the current design (option A, a rigid V frame whose grip rises with the load through its lever arm) sits within known physics, and so do its strength (after one fix), lowering, heat, fit and tether claims. Of the 32 claims for option A, **19 are within known science and 13 are plausible, to confirm at TRL 4**. The plausible rows are the friction of rubber and sleeved chain on wet coconut bark (S3, the claim the lock rests on), the slack drop (S2), settlement with pad shear (S5), wear of the slot faces (S10), the rigging time and the time to the ground (S17, S23), the bark under the pads' lower edge on green wood (S19, S21), the victim turning head up (S22), corrosion and ageing (S25, S26) and a leaning trunk (S31). The row that failed is the strength margin: the frame's estimated breaking load is about 5.0 kN, 5.1:1 on the one-person load, and about 4.5:1 if the chain's whole pull falls on one slot, below the 5:1 to 10:1 that rope rescue teams aim for (S30); raising it costs carried mass, a safety trade-off put to Amish (TKB-DEC-001 O7). Amish decided on 2026-10-25 ("i concur with your recommendations") to keep the 2.5 kN design load, raise the TRL 4 proof load to 3.5 kN and decide after a test to failure of a sample frame, with the stronger frame as the fallback below 5:1, so S30 is now plausible, to confirm by that test. For the leaning trunk (S31) he set a 20 deg lean limit in PR-003 and a drill rule for the frame's side (O8). `stem_check` is **open**. Two details failed and were fixed under Amish's standing instruction of 2026-10-23: the tether quick link did not pass a 6 mm chain link (D-S1, S27, S28), and the independent review showed that the 35 mm cleat web failed if the chain's pull fell on one slot, so the spine top is raised 20 mm to a 55 mm web (D-S2, S9). Options B and C have no failing row; each rests on a plausible claim that only a test could settle. Method: STANDARDS section 20. Calculations: `docs/04-calcs/stem_check.py`, which imports the calculation note's script (`docs/04-calcs/sizing.py`, TKB-CAL-001) and so the model.

> **Safety:** TrunkBelay holds and lowers a person at height. A verdict of "within known science" means the claim is consistent with physics on paper, not that the kit is safe. Nobody is lowered with it before the collar has been proof-tested at 2.5 kN on cut trunk sections and the drill has been run with a dummy at low height (TKB-BLD-001, section 6).

## Fields not relevant

None. Every field is touched: physics (statics of the self-locking couple, strength of the frame, chain and rope, energy and heat of the lowering, bark contact), chemistry (corrosion of the bare aluminium frame, ageing of polymers), biology (bark and stem tissue of the palm, the body of the person lowered, the rescuer's work at height), mathematics (units, orders of magnitude, the numerical method of the lock and the chain path) and engineering (mass budget, fit of bought parts, manufacturing tolerances).

## Concept options

- **Option A, self-locking lever collar** (the current design): rows S1 to S32; 19 within known science, 12 plausible, 1 fails with a fix proposed to Amish (S30, strength margin). The lock (S3) is the deciding unknown.
- **Option B, constricting wrap anchor**: within known science on strength and bark pressure (B2, B3). Its holding claim (B1) is plausible on a capstan estimate (about 3.8 times the load with four wraps and an assumed friction of 0.3), but how a friction hitch tightens round a large, wet, tapering trunk under an axial pull is not covered by a cited source; the rigging time (B4) is an estimate. Not dropped.
- **Option C, screwed stem anchor**: the lug is within known science (C3). The screws' hold (C1) rests on timber formulas that do not apply to palm stem tissue; the permanent holes (C2) fail PR-008 by design; driving four screws by hand at height (C4) is estimated at about 8 minutes, against 5 for the whole rig. Not dropped on the STEM check; it scores lowest in TKB-OPT-001.
